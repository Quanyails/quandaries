# Q&A

Hey Katie!

I'm glad you've been enjoying the puzzles I've been submitting! Out of the publications I've worked with in my crossword journey, the LAT team has been my favorite to work with. I think you do a good job of bringing the best out of my ideas! Let me know what you end up deciding on for the fill.

> PS: I hope you don't mind my asking, but I'm curious about your experience as a constructor and how you got started! You are very talented at making clean, sparkly grids, and your themes are super fun, so I'd love to hear about your crossword origin story :) 

No problem, happy to share!


**Why crosswords interest me**

I've been a fan of word trivia since I can remember. Back in middle and high school, I'd spend after-hours browsing material in this vein, including articles on [Wikipedia's list of unusual articles][unusual-articles], [Mike Keith's blog][mike-keith], and the puzzles in the pages of *[Gödel, Escher, Bach][godel-escher-bach]*.

I wouldn't be surprised if this aspect came about through my heritage. [Chinese is a language with a lot of wordplay baked in][chinese-wordlplay], so it's natural to find wordplay interesting in a different language.

In addition to the wordplay aspect, I enjoy novelty. If I can discover a clever connection and share it with the world, I feel like I contributed something to the science of puzzle-making.

For reasons like these, crossword puzzles seemed like a natural fit for me. 


**How I got started**

I discovered crossword puzzles thanks to the librarians at middle school. Every day, they would print out a few copies of the NYT crossword for students to play. Even if I could only complete most of them by consulting Google, I found crosswords quite fun.

Around high school, I discovered [Diary of a Crossword Fiend][crossword-fiend]. This blog opened up the "world of crosswords" for me. Instead of crosswords being a solitary, one-and-done-style activity, I found people who would write and analyze these crosswords. Reading the blog posts made me understand that crossword constructors were their own beast, and I came to admire how creative a lot of the themes on the blog were.

At the time, constructing my own crosswords seemed out of the question. For one, I couldn't complete the harder NYT puzzles, so I doubt I had the lexicon to compete with that of worldly, educated adults. In addition, the only professional crossword construction software at the time was paid, and my parents weren't the type to spend money on this sort of activity.

In college, I kept crosswords as a hobby "on simmer". The most I engaged with the hobby was when I was traveling pre-COVID, where I'd load up [Alphacross][alphacross] with the latest crosswords and complete them on my plane flights.

Once I graduated from college, my interest in crosswords picked back up. At my first full-time job (Duolingo), there was a crossword club that would meet during lunch and complete the NYT together (shout out to [Art Chaidarun][art-chaidarun]). At my next (Google), the site lead for my office was a fan of crosswords, and we played a mini crossword he designed as a game in one of our meetings.

The Duolingo crossword club reinvigorated my interest in crosswords. The "problem" I had with solving crosswords is how I played them: If I got stuck, I got stuck. It was unsatisfying having to look up answers to complete a crossword. Playing crosswords collaboratively made the hobby much more enjoyable—not just in completing the crossword but also as a way to socialize. Outside of work, I ended up solving crosswords collaboratively through [Discord][discord].

From there, one of my friends decided to make his own crossword. Now, at this point, I had known indie crossword construction apps like [Crosshare][crosshare] existed, but I had always been under the impression that these apps were just that—for indie venues. Noticing my friend could just make a crossword online now, though, made me realize, "Hey! I'm one of these worldly, educated adults now (I hope)! And I have money! I can make crosswords of my own, too!"

From here, I read up on the latest online resources on how to make a crossword. I started by making some midi-sized puzzles in [Crossworthy][crossworthy]. I then asked a few Duolingo friends for advice + resources on crossword construction, and with their suggestions, I was able to bump up the sparkliness of my constructed puzzles from that at a hobbyist level to that aiming for publication.

I don't think there's any shame in admitting that getting a NYT acceptance was the ultimate goal for an aspiring crossword constructor like myself. Along the way, though, I learned what it was like working with editors for other publications. The LAT was one of the crosswords in Alphacross I enjoyed more often than not, and their submission process seemed reasonable, so I started submitting my crosswords there. And hey! On my fourth 15x15 construction, I got an acceptance.

I still consider myself far from great at crossword construction! I prioritize fresh ideas, and given I'm picking up a hobby that has existed (as famously thought) since 1913, you have to be inventive to come up with something undiscovered. There's a lot I have left to learn. The learning process can be slow, but I'd like to think that every rejection with feedback brings me closer in line with what editors are looking for.

I recognize that this anecdote doesn't answer the question about how I make "clean, sparkly grids" or fun themes, so let me explain that too!


**How I make crosswords**

*Regarding filling grids:*

The method I use to fill crosswords is, from what I've seen, unique among crossword constructors: My "personal wordlist" is configured so that each entry in the wordlist isn't directly scored but is associated with any number of tags, which receive their own scores. By manipulating tag scores alongside the global minimum score in [Ingrid][ingrid], I can find the right balance of fill for a given grid. E.g., if I see that the fill for a grid is starting to pile up on acronyms/names/crosswordese, I can filter out the appropriate tag from Ingrid's autofill partway through the fill attempt.

Furthermore, this tagging system offers flexibility in other ways. Different venues hold crossword puzzles to different requirements, and I can use tags to adjust my wordlist depending on which venue a given crossword is targeting. E.g., for a crossword designed with the LAT in mind, I can create a low-scoring tag for "bad vibes" and tag the relevant entries. Then, for a non-LAT publication, I can upscore the tag. As an example, I downscored GRENADE when filling this crossword in favor of GRENADA. Using a tagging system lets me be more systematic in the quality of the fill.

In tandem with this method, I'm pretty unforgiving when it comes to fill quality. I'll spend hours or even days on a 15x15 grid looking for the configuration that gives me the best fill. It's time-consuming for sure, but it's quite satisfying feeling like you didn't need to make compromises.

For these reasons, I find I can often work with themes that require a high theme density. For a 15x15 crossword, I would say the upper limit on # of theme squares is in the mid-50s.


*Regarding themes:*

One of my top priorities in making crosswords is "fun". If a theme doesn't tickle me, it's unlikely to tickle the editors of the publications I'm submitting the puzzle to. That said, I am a nerd, so I have my biases in what I consider "fun".

In both crosswords and other hobbies, I think about the solver's perspective over the constructor's, and I think about whether my playtesters (who are casual solvers, not other crossword constructors) enjoy the puzzle. This also means that themes that are feats of construction but are a slog to solve for are ones that I don't find fun to work through.

Novelty helps make a theme feel fun to me. If a solver can complete my puzzle purely by examining previous crosswords, I'm not engaging them (within reason—I don't make things novel for the sake of novelty). If the theme I'm brainstorming occupies well-worn territory, I'd like to find something new to share. E.g., [the POLES APART puzzle I submitted][poles-apart] uses a fairly typical bookends theme, but BARBENHEIMER being a fresh, in-the-language theme entry elevated the theme from "fine" to "I really want to get BARBENHEIMER in the annals of crossword history."
